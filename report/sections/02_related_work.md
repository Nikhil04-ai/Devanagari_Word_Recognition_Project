# 2. Related work

**Sequence recognition with CNN, RNN and CTC.** Reading a word as a sequence of image columns avoids cutting it into characters. The CRNN of Shi et al. [1] combines a convolutional feature extractor, a recurrent layer and Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC) [5], which trains on unsegmented labels by summing over all alignments between the output sequence and the transcription. It is trained end to end, needs no character segmentation and is not tied to a lexicon [1]; it is our Model B.

**Transformer OCR.** TrOCR [2] removes both the CNN backbone and the recurrent layer: an image Transformer encoder, built like ViT [6] and DeiT [7], encodes image patches with self-attention, and a text Transformer decoder generates the transcription token by token. Both halves start from pre-trained models; the small configuration we use pairs a DeiT-small encoder with a MiniLM decoder and has 62 million parameters [2]. It is pre-trained on large amounts of synthetic text images and fine-tuned on labelled data, and its authors report state-of-the-art results on printed, handwritten and scene text [2]. Its vocabulary is English word pieces, so we adapt it to Devanagari characters (Section 6).

**Devanagari and Indic handwriting.** Early Devanagari recognisers segmented a word into characters or recognised whole words from a fixed lexicon, and every paper used a different dataset [3]. Dutta et al. [3] addressed this with IIIT-HW-Dev: 95,381 word images from 12 writers, with a vocabulary of 9,540 words and train, validation and test splits that share no writer. They benchmarked a CNN-RNN hybrid with CTC, close to the CRNN but with residual convolution blocks and a spatial transformer. Trained only on IIIT-HW-Dev and without the spatial transformer, it reached 17.56 % CER and 45.72 % WER on the test set; adding the spatial transformer and pre-training on one million synthetic Devanagari word images and on English handwriting (IAM) lowered this to 8.64 % CER and 26.22 % WER without a lexicon [3]. These numbers were measured on the original release (95,381 images, segmentation not manually corrected [3]); we use Version 1 (95,430 images), which corrects segmentation errors (Section 3.2), so they are context, not a direct comparison. Gongidi and Jawahar [8] later extended the collection to IIIT-INDIC-HW-WORDS, with 868K word images from 135 writers in eight further scripts, so that, together with IIIT-HW-Dev, word-level handwriting data exists for ten Indic scripts. At character level, the DHCD dataset [4] provides 92,000 images of 46 isolated consonants and digits.

**Low-resource handwriting recognition.** Deep recognisers have millions of parameters and need many labelled images, which few scripts have. Two remedies recur in the work above: generating synthetic training images in the target script [3], and transferring a model trained on a large dataset in another script [3], [8]. Gongidi and Jawahar [8] find that pre-training helps most when the target training set is small, that its benefit shrinks as more real data is added, and that pre-training on a visually similar script is not clearly better than pre-training on English. TrOCR's pre-training [2] is the same idea at a larger scale.

**Position of this work.** We propose no new method: we compare three standard model families on one script with the same metrics and splits, without synthetic or extra data, and ask whether a Transformer pre-trained only on English handwriting transfers to Devanagari.
