# 2. Related work

*Owner: Monu. Every cited paper was opened; see `references.md` for what was checked in each.*

**Sequence recognition with CNN, RNN and CTC.** Reading a word as a sequence of image columns avoids cutting it into characters. The CRNN of Shi et al. [1] combines a convolutional feature extractor, a recurrent layer and Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC) [5], which trains on unsegmented labels by summing over all alignments between the output sequence and the transcription. The authors stress four properties: the network is trained end to end, it handles sequences of any length without character segmentation, it is not tied to a lexicon, and it is much smaller than earlier models [1]. It is the sequence-recognition baseline of this project (Model B).

**Transformer OCR.** TrOCR [2] removes both the CNN backbone and the recurrent layer. An image Transformer encoder, built like ViT [6] and DeiT [7], splits the image into patches and encodes them with self-attention; a text Transformer decoder generates the transcription one token at a time and attends to the image through cross-attention. Both halves start from pre-trained models: the encoder from DeiT or BEiT, the decoder from RoBERTa or MiniLM. The small configuration we use pairs a DeiT-small encoder with a MiniLM decoder and has 62 million parameters [2]. The model is pre-trained on large amounts of synthetic text images and then fine-tuned on labelled data, and its authors report that it outperforms the previous state of the art on printed, handwritten and scene text [2]. Its vocabulary is English word pieces, so we adapt it to Devanagari characters (Section 6).

**Devanagari and Indic handwriting.** Early Devanagari recognisers either segmented a word into characters or zones and classified the pieces, or recognised whole words from a fixed lexicon; recurrent networks removed both the segmentation step and the fixed lexicon [3]. Public word-level data was scarce and every paper used a different dataset, which made methods hard to compare. Dutta et al. [3] addressed this with IIIT-HW-Dev: 95,381 word images from 12 writers, with a vocabulary of 9,540 words and train, validation and test splits that share no writer. They benchmarked a CNN-RNN hybrid with CTC, close to the CRNN but with residual convolution blocks and a spatial transformer. Trained only on IIIT-HW-Dev and without the spatial transformer, it reached 17.56 % CER and 45.72 % WER on the test set; adding the spatial transformer and pre-training on one million synthetic Devanagari word images and on English handwriting (IAM) lowered this to 8.64 % CER and 26.22 % WER without a lexicon [3]. These numbers were measured on the original release (95,381 images, segmentation not manually corrected [3]); we use Version 1 (95,430 images), which corrects segmentation errors (Section 3.2), so they are context, not a direct comparison. Gongidi and Jawahar [8] later extended the collection to IIIT-INDIC-HW-WORDS, with 868K word images from 135 writers in eight further scripts, so that, together with IIIT-HW-Dev, word-level handwriting data exists for ten Indic scripts. At character level, the DHCD dataset [4] provides 92,000 images of 46 isolated consonants and digits.

**Low-resource handwriting recognition.** Deep recognisers have millions of parameters and need many labelled images, which few scripts have. Two remedies recur in the work above: generating synthetic training images in the target script [3], and transferring a model trained on a large dataset in another script [3], [8]. Gongidi and Jawahar [8] find that pre-training helps most when the target training set is small, that its benefit shrinks as more real data is added, and that pre-training on a visually similar script is not clearly better than pre-training on English. TrOCR's pre-training [2] is the same idea at a larger scale.

**Position of this work.** We propose no new method: we compare three standard model families on one script with the same metrics and splits, without synthetic or extra data, and ask whether a Transformer pre-trained only on English handwriting transfers to Devanagari.
